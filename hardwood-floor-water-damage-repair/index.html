Hardwood floor water damage is the swelling, cupping, crowning or staining of wood and laminate flooring after it absorbs moisture. Wood is a sponge that expands across the grain, and it releases water slowly, so a floor that looks fine after a leak can start to cup a day or two later.

If a floor is wet right now, stop the source, lift rugs and furniture legs off the wood and call the 24/7 dispatch line. Do not sand or refinish anything until the wood and the subfloor read dry, because sealing in moisture makes the problem worse.

Cupping, crowning and buckling: what each one means

The shape of the damage tells a story about where the water was. Cupping means the edges are higher than the center, which usually points to moisture from below. Crowning is the reverse, with a raised center, and often follows a cupped floor that was sanded before it dried.

Buckling is the serious one, when boards lift off the subfloor. That usually means a large amount of water or a long exposure, and the floor often has to be removed in sections.

Cupping after a leak: the call we hear most

The typical story is a washer valve, a dishwasher line or a pipe in the ceiling below a bathroom that leaked quietly. Days later the boards near the spot start to lift. By then the water has long since stopped and the floor is still wet underneath.

The fix starts with finding the moisture, not the boards. We scan the floor surface and probe the subfloor, because a floor can read dry on top while the plywood underneath is still saturated.

Why drying a wood floor takes patience

Drying too fast cracks boards, and drying too slowly invites mold. Crews use controlled airflow, low-humidity air and sometimes floor drying mats that pull moisture through the boards from the surface.

Readings are taken at each visit. We compare wood moisture to a dry reference from the same floor, because the right number differs between homes and species. When the numbers stabilize, we tell you whether sanding, replacement or nothing is the right next step.

Laminate and engineered flooring

Laminate has a fiberboard core that swells at the seams and does not recover, so water that sits for more than a short time usually means replacement. Engineered wood has a plywood core and tolerates a little more, but edges still lift.

If the wet area is large, the carpet page covers the pad and carpet questions in the adjoining rooms, and structural drying explains the equipment used under floors.

First steps on a wet wood floor

Before the crew arrives

  • Stop the water source and wipe standing water with towels.
  • Lift rugs and furniture legs, and put foil or plastic under heavy pieces.
  • Keep the heat on and run air conditioning if it is warm, but do not point a fan across the floor alone.
  • Do not use heat guns or irons to dry boards.
  • Photograph the floor from several angles before anything is moved.

Subfloor and crawl space questions

Many floors sit over plywood on joists, and some sit over a crawl space or a slab. Moisture from below shows up as cupping, so we check what is under the floor as well as the floor itself.

If a crawl space is damp, drying the boards alone will not hold. The source has to be found, and the readings will show whether the floor keeps taking on moisture after the first round of drying.
