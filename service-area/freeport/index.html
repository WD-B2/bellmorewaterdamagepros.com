Water damage restoration in Freeport, NY has a local pattern that does not match a typical inland suburb. The village is cut by canals and salt marsh on its south side, so water reaches Freeport streets and basements from the bay as well as from the sky and from broken pipes.

When water is spreading in Freeport

Freeport water emergencies come in three kinds. A pressurized pipe breaks and keeps running. Tidal or storm water comes in from the south and pools in basements. Or a sewer line surcharges and pushes dirty water up through floor drains. Each needs a different first move.

For a broken pipe, close the main valve and call us. For tidal or storm water, stay out of the water and call the same line, because standing water can carry sewage and hide electrical hazards. Freeport has a village-owned electric utility, so if water has reached a meter, service panel or outlets, call 911 or the utility before you step into the room.

We do not promise an exact arrival time, but we do offer same-day response when available, and the line is answered at night, on weekends and on holidays.

Flood coverage and your FEMA flood zone

The village flood page says a property may be in or next to a FEMA special flood hazard area, and the Building Department can give your flood zone and tell you whether an elevation certificate is on file. That is worth knowing before a loss, not after.

The same page and the New York Department of Financial Services both say a typical homeowners policy does not cover flood damage. A separate policy through the National Flood Insurance Program may. Sewer or water backup may be covered by an endorsement. Check your policy or ask your insurer, and photograph the damage before any cleanup begins.

Which services fit Freeport water problems

Given the tidal and storm record, the pages most Freeport property owners need first are flood damage restoration, sewage backup cleanup and basement water damage cleanup. Standing water that has to come out immediately is covered by water extraction.

A flooded Freeport home usually needs drying after the water is out, and the readings decide when equipment leaves. If wet drywall or insulation sat for days, ask about mold before rebuilding, because mold needs moisture and removal follows ANSI/IICRC S520 as the published standard.

If water is still spreading: before we arrive

Stop the source

Close the main valve if a pipe is running. If the water is coming from outside, do not wade in to find the cause.

Cut power only if safe

Turn off power to wet areas only if the panel is dry and reachable. If water has reached the panel, call 911 or the utility.

Lift what you can

Raise valuables, paper and electronics off the floor, and put foil or plastic under furniture legs.

Keep people and pets out

Keep everyone out of standing water, especially if it came up through drains or from the street.

Document everything

Photograph the water line, damaged items and the source before anything is moved.

Village water, village drains and village permits

Freeport runs its own water department. The village says it draws water from eight operating wells in the Magothy Aquifer at roughly 500 to 640 feet, stores it in two elevated tanks and moves it through about 125 miles of piping to roughly 10,000 service connections. If a service line or meter is the problem, the water department takes questions on water, sewer and drainage at 516-377-2379.

The village Building and Zoning Department says most construction, plumbing and electrical work needs a permit, and it can be reached at 516-377-2200 or at Village Hall on North Ocean Avenue. After a flood, permit rules for rebuilding vary, so ask the Building Department before ordering materials.

Freeport housing, streets and what that means for damage

The 2020 Census counted 14,137 housing units in Freeport, in a village of about 4.6 square miles. Sunrise Highway and Merrick Road run east and west through it, and the Meadowbrook State Parkway forms its eastern edge. That is a lot of households in a small footprint, and a coastal flood warning reaches most of them at the same time.

The NWS recorded about 3 feet of water at the north end of the Nautical Mile during Hurricane Irene, so anyone near Woodcleft Canal should treat ground-floor space as the first to flood. Moving contents above floor level before a coastal flood warning protects more than any cleanup afterward.
