Sewage backup cleanup is the removal and sanitizing of contaminated water that has come back up through floor drains, toilets or tubs, along with the materials it touched. It is an emergency, because contaminated water spreads and soaks in quickly. Our 24/7 line is open, and while you wait you should keep people and pets out, stop using sinks, tubs and toilets, and avoid touching the water.

If the water has reached outlets or the electrical panel, do not walk in to reach it. Call 911 or your utility before the restoration line, and cut power only if you can reach the panel dry. Photograph the area from the doorway and do not try to mop it up yourself.

Gurgling drains and water coming up: signs of a sewage backup

People rarely search for the trade term. They search for a toilet that bubbles when the washer drains, a floor drain that gurgles and then fills, water rising in a shower after someone flushes upstairs or a smell like a septic tank in the basement.

When wastewater comes back into a house, restoration pros call it a Category 3 loss. That label matters, because it changes how we protect ourselves, what we remove and how we sanitize, and it is the reason we do not treat it like a clean supply leak.

Sewage backup: what to do first

Safe steps right now

  • Keep everyone out of the affected area, including pets, until the cleanup is done.
  • Stop using toilets, sinks, tubs and the washing machine so you do not add more water.
  • Do not touch the water, and do not run a household vacuum on it.
  • Turn off power to the area only if the panel is dry and reachable. Otherwise call the utility or 911.
  • Take photos from the doorway of the floor, the drain and the water line on walls.

Why this is not ordinary water

Clean supply water, gray water and sewage are different categories, and they do not get the same response. Category 3 water carries contamination that can stay in porous materials after the water is gone, so the job is about removing what cannot be cleaned as much as drying what can.

We use protective equipment, containment where needed and antimicrobial treatment after removal. The published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard describes water categories, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it.

What can be saved and what cannot

The rule is simple. Porous material that touched sewage is usually removed, and hard surfaces can usually be cleaned.

MaterialUsually saved?Why
Carpet and padNoFibers absorb contaminated water and cannot be reliably sanitized
Drywall and insulationNo, up to the water line and abovePorous and wicks upward
Tile and sealed concreteOften yesHard surfaces can be cleaned and treated
Wood framingOften yes after cleaning and dryingDense material, assessed with readings
Upholstered furniture and paperNoSoft goods hold contamination

How the job runs from first call to clean

We confirm the source and the safety risk by phone, then dispatch a crew with protective gear. Standing water goes out with extraction units, and unsalvageable materials are removed and bagged. We clean and treat what remains, and then dry it with air movers and dehumidifiers.

Moisture readings and photos are recorded as we go, so your insurer sees what was removed and why. If the backup came with rising water or a storm, the same crew may also be dealing with flood damage cleanup. If it filled a lower level, the steps for basement water damage cleanup apply to the drying that follows.

What causes a backup, and who fixes it

Backups come from a blocked lateral, a failing line, tree roots in the pipe or a municipal sewer problem. Finding and clearing the cause is a plumbing or utility job, and we do not claim to do it.

The local pattern is easy to see. Press reports from Hurricane Sandy in 2012 describe several feet of water in South Bellmore, and Newbridge Creek flooded Linden Street in 2020. After storms like those, ask a plumber whether a backwater valve makes sense, and ask your insurer about backup coverage.
