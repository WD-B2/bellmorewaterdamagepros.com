Emergency water extraction is the removal of standing water from a home or building with submersible pumps, wet vacuums and portable or truck-mounted extraction units. If water is pooled on your floor in Bellmore tonight, the 24/7 line is open, and what you do before a crew arrives decides how much has to be torn out later.

Stop the source if you safely can and keep everyone out of deep water. Do not walk into a room where water has reached outlets, cords or the breaker panel. Cut power only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water, and call 911 or your utility first if there is any electrical or gas risk. Then call the dispatch line and tell us how deep the water is.

When standing water is an emergency

Treat any water deeper than the baseboard, or any water that is still rising, as an emergency. A shallow film left overnight wicks into drywall, swells carpet pad and slips under flooring, and each of those materials gets harder to save with every hour that passes.

The numbers explain why speed matters. One inch of water over a 1,000 square foot floor is roughly 620 gallons, and that weight does not stay on the surface. It moves into every porous material it touches.

Dirty water changes the job. If the water came from a toilet overflow, a sewer line or outdoor flooding, do not handle it bare-handed and do not run a household vacuum in it. Sewage backup cleanup follows stricter safety steps than clean supply water.

Electrical and gas safety comes first

Stay out of any room where water touches outlets, power strips, appliances or the breaker panel. Turn off power only if the panel is dry and reachable. If you smell gas or see sparks, leave the house and call 911 or your utility before you call us.

What extraction actually removes

Extraction is not the same as drying. It takes out the bulk water, the part you can see and the part trapped in carpet and pad, so the drying equipment that follows has far less to do. When we arrive we check depth, find drains and low spots, and decide which machine goes where.

Extraction ends when we can no longer pull loose water from the material. What remains is moisture inside walls, framing and subfloor, which is a drying job that starts with structural drying and dehumidification.

Three common situations and how each is handled

Several inches of standing water

Pumps go in first, with power off and hazards checked. The goal is to get the level below the baseboards quickly so the walls stop absorbing from the bottom.

A shallow film across a hard floor

Wet vacuums and squeegee heads handle it, and speed matters because water creeps under baseboards and into floor seams.

A soaked carpet and pad

The wand pulls water from the carpet, and the pad underneath often comes out because it holds water like a sponge and dries slowly.

Water in a finished lower level

We check what is stored at floor level, lift what can be saved and remove what cannot, then extract from the lowest point outward.

Bellmore low ground and water that comes back up the drains

South Bellmore, the area south of Merrick Road, has a record of water arriving from the street instead of the roof. Local press reported that during Hurricane Sandy in 2012 a tidal surge pushed water back through storm drains, and that several feet of water stood in parts of South Bellmore. In 2014, reports described about five inches of rain in six hours with water at Newbridge Road and Sunrise Highway.

The practical point is simple. Water that arrives from drains or from the street should be treated as dirty until shown otherwise, so tell the dispatcher where it came from. That decides whether we set up for clean-water extraction or contaminated-water protocols. Check the FEMA flood map for your address as well.

While you wait for the crew

  • Photograph the water line on walls and furniture before anything moves. Your insurer will want dated photos.
  • Lift small items, rugs and electronics onto counters or upper shelves, and put foil or plastic under furniture legs.
  • Keep children and pets out of the water, especially if it may have come from a drain.
  • Leave wet carpet in place unless a crew tells you otherwise, because it helps us see how far the water spread.
  • Write down when the water started and what you think caused it.
